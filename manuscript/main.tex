\begin{abstract}
Whether a place on Earth remains habitable is not decided by any single
environmental variable in isolation: it is the joint outcome of atmospheric
warming, of the ocean's changing heat content and carbonate chemistry, and of
the surface hydrosphere that governs water availability. Yet the dominant
quantitative frameworks treat these spheres separately. The human-climate-niche
and heat-stress literatures reduce habitability to temperature alone; ocean
acidification is assessed in its own community; and the influential Planetary
Boundaries framework, while explicitly multi-variable, defines each boundary as a
\emph{global, aggregate} control variable evaluated independently, with no
spatial field and no representation of how the boundaries compound on the same
piece of land. None of these can answer the operational question a policymaker
actually faces---\emph{where}, and by how much, does habitability change as the
Earth system is pushed. Here we argue that this is not a gap to be patched but a
paradigm to be shifted: habitability must be modelled as an inherently
\emph{multi-sphere}, spatially explicit, and observationally calibrated quantity.
We operationalise this with a per-cell Composite Hazard Score (CHS) that
integrates six carried climate--ocean variables with a real gridded surface-water
hazard field, and whose area-weighted, baseline-referenced integral is the
Habitable Area Fraction (HAF). A two-layer energy-balance climate core is
calibrated against HadCRUT5 surface temperature (1850--1980) and NOAA/NCEI ocean
heat content (2005--2020) by tempered Sequential Monte Carlo, yielding an
equilibrium climate sensitivity of $3.69$\,K (5--95\%: $3.03$--$4.31$) and
positive out-of-sample skill over a withheld 1981--2020 window. The multi-sphere
HAF declines from $0.90$ (preindustrial) to $0.78$ (2020) and, by 2100, to
$0.73$, $0.63$, $0.36$, and $0.21$ under SSP1-2.6 through SSP5-8.5. A formal
decomposition shows the emissions pathway dominates the outcome by roughly an
order of magnitude over parametric and weighting uncertainty combined. The
framework is fully reproducible from public data and is designed so that each
sphere's representation can be upgraded independently---turning habitability
assessment from a collection of single-sphere indicators into one integrated,
falsifiable, and mappable metric.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}
Habitability is a whole-system property. A region becomes less liveable not
because one threshold is crossed but because warming, water stress, and the
degradation of marine resources act together on the same population at the same
time\citep{Xu2020, ArmstrongMcKay2022}. This is a statement about \emph{spheres}:
the atmosphere sets temperature and the greenhouse forcing; the ocean stores the
great majority of the added heat and, through its carbonate chemistry, governs
marine calcifiers and food security; and the surface hydrosphere---groundwater,
aridity, irrigation, river regime---decides whether the land can actually support
life and agriculture. These spheres are physically coupled, but scientifically
they are studied apart, and the tools we use to summarise planetary habitability
inherit that separation.

Three families of tools dominate, and each is single-sphere by construction.
First, the human-climate-niche and lethal-heat literatures collapse habitability
onto temperature (and at best humidity), producing compelling but
one-dimensional maps that are silent on ocean state and water
availability\citep{Xu2020}. Second, ocean acidification and heat-content
assessments quantify the marine sphere rigorously but in isolation from the land
surface where people live. Third---and most influentially---the Planetary
Boundaries framework\citep{Rockstrom2009, Steffen2015, Richardson2023} tracks nine
Earth-system processes, but each boundary is a \emph{single global aggregate}
(one number for climate, one for freshwater, one for ocean acidification),
assessed independently of the others and carrying no spatial structure. It tells
us how many boundaries humanity has transgressed globally; it cannot tell us
which \emph{places} lose habitability, nor how the transgressions of several
boundaries compound within a single grid cell. The consequence is a field that
has mastered the parts and lacks an operational model of the whole.

We contend that closing this gap requires more than adding variables to an
existing index; it requires treating habitability as a genuinely multi-sphere,
spatial, and calibrated quantity from the outset. Concretely, a habitability
metric should (i) carry variables from more than one sphere and let them compound
locally; (ii) live on a real geographic grid so that loss of habitability has a
location; and (iii) be tied to observations through a probabilistic calibration
so that its projections are falsifiable and carry honest uncertainty. No existing
operational framework satisfies all three simultaneously. Building one is the aim
of this work, and we regard it as a deliberate paradigm shift rather than an
incremental extension.

We implement this program for the shallow-time (industrial-era to 2300\,CE)
regime, where instrumental observations permit genuine calibration. A per-cell
Composite Hazard Score (CHS) integrates six carried climate--ocean variables---
global-mean surface temperature, atmospheric CO$_2$, sea-surface temperature,
ocean heat content, surface-ocean pH, and aragonite saturation---onto a real
$0.5^{\circ}$ land grid, together with a \emph{real gridded surface-water hazard
field} that supplies the emergent spatial structure. The area-weighted,
baseline-referenced integral of the CHS below a habitability threshold is the
Habitable Area Fraction (HAF). The climate--ocean state is generated by a
two-layer energy-balance emulator whose two free parameters are calibrated by
tempered Sequential Monte Carlo against surface temperature and ocean heat
content, with a decade of temperature observations withheld for out-of-sample
validation. Everything below is regenerated by a single seeded pipeline from
public data.

\section*{A multi-sphere habitability metric}
The CHS is a tiered per-cell score (Methods; full construction in the
Supplementary Information). Each carried variable is oriented so that larger
means more hazardous, anchored to its preindustrial baseline, and scaled to a
common reference so that scenarios and posterior draws are comparable. The score
on land cell $c$ at time $t$ separates into three tiers,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CHS}(c,t) = \underbrace{P(c)\,s_T(t)}_{\text{(i) climate pattern}}
 + \underbrace{B(c)}_{\text{(ii) surface-water hazard}}
 + \underbrace{U(t)}_{\text{(iii) ocean / global modifiers}},
\end{equation}
where tier~(i) is the surface-temperature driver projected onto a present-day
land-warming pattern $P(c)$ (atmospheric sphere); tier~(ii) is a fixed baseline
field $B(c)$ carrying the geography of surface-water hazard; and tier~(iii)
collects the spatially uniform ocean and global-mean contributions (oceanic
sphere). Crucially, $B(c)$ is not a stylised function of latitude: it is a
\emph{real} gridded Water Hazard Index---integrating groundwater stress, climatic
aridity, irrigation pressure, river-stage sensitivity, and irreversible aquifer
compaction---resampled to the model grid (92\% land coverage; the remainder
filled with the land median). This is what makes the metric multi-sphere in
substance rather than in name: the hydrosphere enters as observed geography, the
ocean enters through four carbonate and heat variables, and the atmosphere enters
through the calibrated warming field. The HAF is the cosine-latitude area-weighted
fraction of land whose CHS stays below $\tau$, the 90th percentile of the pooled
preindustrial CHS distribution, so that $\mathrm{HAF}\!\approx\!0.9$ by
construction in the preindustrial and subsequent decline measures genuine loss.

\section*{Calibrating the climate--ocean core}
The climate--ocean state that drives the CHS comes from a two-layer
(Geoffroy-type) energy-balance emulator\citep{Geoffroy2013} forced by IPCC~AR6
effective radiative forcing\citep{Forster2021} extended along the SSP
pathways\citep{Meinshausen2020, oneill2017roads}. Its two free parameters---the
equilibrium climate sensitivity (ECS) and the ocean heat-uptake coefficient
$\gamma$---are calibrated by tempered Sequential Monte Carlo\citep{DelMoral2006}
against HadCRUT5 global-mean surface temperature over 1850--1980\citep{Morice2021}
and NOAA/NCEI 0--2000\,m ocean heat content over 2005--2020\citep{Levitus2012},
under a robust Student-$t$ likelihood. The decade-plus of surface-temperature
observations after 1980 is deliberately withheld for out-of-sample validation.

The posterior is physically credible and observationally sharp
(Fig.~\ref{fig:posterior}): ECS\,$=3.69$\,K with a 5--95\% range of
$3.03$--$4.31$\,K, squarely within the AR6 assessed range, and
$\gamma=0.54$\,W\,m$^{-2}$\,K$^{-1}$ ($0.36$--$0.79$). The calibrated emulator
reproduces observed warming through the calibration window and, critically, over
the withheld 1981--2020 window (Fig.~\ref{fig:gmst}): the out-of-sample
root-mean-square error is $0.21$\,K, a skill of $+0.43$ against a persistence
baseline and $+0.58$ against a fitted linear trend---that is, the model
extrapolates, it does not merely interpolate its training data. It simultaneously
matches the 2005--2020 ocean-heat-content rise to within $8$\,ZJ
(Fig.~S1). Jointly fitting surface temperature \emph{and} ocean heat
is what lifts the ECS--$\gamma$ degeneracy that afflicts temperature-only
calibrations, and it is only possible because the emulator carries the ocean
sphere explicitly.

\section*{Habitability declines steeply, and the pathway decides how far}
The multi-sphere HAF tells a clear and readable story (Fig.~\ref{fig:haf}). In
the preindustrial the HAF sits at $0.90$ by construction. It has already fallen to
$0.78$ by 2020---a $12$-percentage-point loss of habitable land area attributable
to observed warming, ocean change, and the present-day water-hazard geography
acting together. Its future then fans out sharply by emissions pathway. By 2100
the HAF is $0.73$ under the strong-mitigation SSP1-2.6, $0.63$ under the
middle-of-the-road SSP2-4.5, $0.36$ under SSP3-7.0, and $0.21$ under the
high-emission SSP5-8.5, tracking end-of-century warming of $1.8$, $3.0$, $4.5$,
and $5.4$\,K above 1850--1900. The divergence widens further to 2300: under the
two lower scenarios the HAF stabilises near $0.75$ and $0.59$, whereas under
SSP3-7.0 and SSP5-8.5 it collapses to $0.01$ and $\sim\!0$---a near-total loss of
land that clears the composite climate--ocean--water threshold. In plain terms,
mitigation does not merely slow the decline; it selects between a world that
retains most of its habitable land and one that loses almost all of it.

Two features of these trajectories deserve emphasis. First, the loss is already
underway (the 2020 value), so the metric is anchored to a present that has
observably moved, not to a hypothetical baseline. Second, the curves are smooth
and monotone in warming but strongly convex across scenarios: the last increments
of warming remove disproportionately more habitable area, because by then the
climate driver is large enough to push even moderate-baseline land over the
threshold.

\section*{Where habitability is lost}
Because the CHS lives on a real grid, it also answers \emph{where}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:chs}). The 2100 CHS map under SSP2-4.5 concentrates hazard in the
subtropical dry belts and continental interiors---the Sahara and Sahel, the
Arabian Peninsula, the Middle East and Central Asia, interior Australia, the
south-western United States and northern Mexico, and Patagonia---while maritime
and high-latitude margins remain comparatively habitable. This pattern is not
imposed by hand: it emerges from the real surface-water hazard field $B(c)$
modulated by the calibrated land-warming pattern. It is precisely the kind of
spatially resolved, compounded-hazard geography that a global aggregate boundary
cannot express, and it is where the multi-sphere construction pays off---the same
map cannot be produced by a temperature-only niche or by a non-spatial index.

\section*{Attributing the uncertainty}
A central advantage of a calibrated, probabilistic framework is that it lets us
ask what actually controls the answer. We decompose the spread in the 2100 HAF
into three sources (Fig.~\ref{fig:haf}; Methods). The \emph{scenario} spread---the
range across the four SSPs---is $0.52$. The \emph{weighting} spread---obtained by
drawing $500$ weight vectors from a flat Dirichlet over the six carried variables
and re-evaluating the HAF---has a 5--95\% width of only $0.072$. The
\emph{parametric} spread---the 5--95\% width from the calibrated
$(\mathrm{ECS},\gamma)$ posterior propagated through the whole chain---is smaller
still, $0.031$. The emissions pathway therefore dominates the outcome by roughly
an order of magnitude over the choice of variable weights and by more than an
order of magnitude over parametric uncertainty. This is a reassuring and
policy-relevant result: the multi-sphere HAF is robust to how the spheres are
weighted and to the residual uncertainty in the climate response, so the
projected loss of habitable land is a statement about human choices, not about
modelling arbitrariness.

\section*{The climate core: why two layers, and the case for coupling the spheres}
The climate engine used here is the two-layer Geoffroy energy-balance
model\citep{Geoffroy2013}, the standard core of FaIR-class
emulators\citep{Leach2021} and the same core used in the perspective from which
this study grows. Its virtues are exactly those needed for calibration: with a
surface (mixed-layer) and a deep-ocean layer it captures the two dominant
timescales of the transient response with just two free parameters, it admits an
exact matrix-exponential solution so the integration is error-free, and its low
dimension makes a full Bayesian calibration tractable. For reproducing the
observed surface-temperature and ocean-heat records, it is fit for purpose.

It is, however, not the end state that a genuinely multi-sphere programme
demands, and it is worth being scientifically explicit about why. The two-layer
model collapses the ocean's continuous spectrum of uptake timescales into a
single exchange coefficient $\gamma$; when surface temperature and ocean heat are
fit jointly, this manifests as a mild trade-off in which a somewhat high ECS is
compensated by a low $\gamma$ (as our posterior shows). A three-layer or $N$-box
ocean\citep{Cummins2020} resolves the intermediate-depth reservoir and reduces
this degeneracy, at the cost of one or two additional calibrated parameters that
the ocean-heat record can now afford to constrain. More fundamentally, in the
present framework the atmospheric CO$_2$ pathway is \emph{prescribed} and the
ocean carbonate variables (pH, aragonite saturation) are \emph{diagnosed} from it
rather than being prognostic states of a coupled system. That is adequate for a
scenario-driven assessment, but it stops short of the multi-sphere ideal: the
atmosphere and ocean spheres are read from a scenario, not integrated together.
The scientifically natural next step---and the one this framework is deliberately
structured to accept---is to replace the two-layer core plus diagnostic chemistry
with a coupled climate--carbon--ocean-chemistry emulator in which CO$_2$, heat,
and the carbonate system evolve as one prognostic multi-sphere state, with the
same tempered-SMC calibration extended to the additional observational
constraints. Because each tier of the CHS and each component of the emulator is
modular, this upgrade changes a component, not the architecture; the HAF, the
calibration protocol, and the uncertainty decomposition carry over unchanged.

\section*{Discussion}
The result we most want to stress is not a single number but a change of frame.
Treating habitability as a global aggregate---the Planetary Boundaries logic---or
as a single sphere---the temperature-niche logic---systematically discards the two
things decision-makers need: a location and a compounded, multi-sphere account of
what is happening there. The framework presented here supplies both, and it does
so while remaining fully calibrated and reproducible. Its headline finding is
that the emissions pathway, not modelling choice, governs how much habitable land
survives the century, and that under high emissions the multi-sphere loss is
near-total by 2300.

We are explicit about scope and limitations. The forcing and concentration
pathways are standard reduced constructions; the surface-water hazard field, while
real and gridded, is a present-day climatology held fixed into the future rather
than evolved; and the ocean chemistry is diagnostic. None of these is a flaw in
the architecture---each is a component that the modular design invites replacing,
and the uncertainty decomposition already shows that the dominant lever
(emissions) sits far above these component choices. What the framework
establishes is that a spatial, calibrated, multi-sphere habitability metric is not
only desirable but buildable and testable today. Making it the default unit of
habitability assessment is, we argue, the paradigm shift the field now needs.

\section*{Methods}
\paragraph*{Climate--ocean emulator.}
Surface and deep-ocean temperature anomalies $(T,T_d)$ evolve as
$C_s\,\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}t = \Delta F(t) - (F_{2\times}/\mathrm{ECS})\,T -
\gamma (T-T_d)$ and $C_d\,\mathrm{d}T_d/\mathrm{d}t = \gamma (T-T_d)$, with
$C_s=7.3$, $C_d=106$\,W\,yr\,m$^{-2}$\,K$^{-1}$ and
$F_{2\times}=3.93$\,W\,m$^{-2}$\citep{Geoffroy2013}. The system is advanced by the
exact matrix exponential (FaIR's energy-balance solver when available, a
deterministic two-box forward-Euler integrator otherwise); ocean heat content is
the ocean's fixed share of the time-integrated net top-of-atmosphere imbalance.
Sea-surface temperature is pattern-scaled from global warming; surface-ocean pH
and aragonite saturation are diagnosed with PyCO2SYS\citep{Humphreys2022} from the
prescribed CO$_2$ pathway and SST.

\paragraph*{Calibration.}
Tempered SMC\citep{DelMoral2006} propagates $400$ particles along a 12-step
inverse-temperature ladder with systematic resampling below $N/2$ effective
particles and Gaussian random-walk Metropolis rejuvenation. Simulated surface
temperature is referenced to the observations' 1850--1900 baseline and ocean heat
content to 2005--2014 before the residuals enter the Student-$t$ likelihood.
Priors are ECS\,$\sim\mathcal{N}(3.0,0.5)$\,K and
$\gamma\sim\mathcal{U}(0.3,1.2)$\,W\,m$^{-2}$\,K$^{-1}$; the run is bit-reproducible
given the seed.

\paragraph*{CHS, HAF, and uncertainty.}
The six carried variables are standardised, tiered, and combined with equal
weight $1/6$; the baseline field $B(c)$ is the real gridded Water Hazard
Index\citep{breiman2001random}. The HAF is evaluated on the $0.5^{\circ}$ Natural
Earth land grid ($259{,}200$ cells, $85{,}959$ land, $29\%$ land area) via a
precomputed HAF$(s_T,U)$ lookup table. Scenario spread is the range over the four
SSPs; the weighting ensemble draws $500$ Dirichlet weight vectors; the parametric
ensemble propagates $200$ posterior draws. Full specifications are given in the
Supplementary Information.
